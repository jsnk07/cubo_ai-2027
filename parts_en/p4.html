Part IV

The AI Challenge

Three weeks to go from a real problem to a documented, tested and defended AI solution. It is the program's practical filter.

Section 9The AI Challenge

Practical selection and execution filter.

The AI Challenge is a three-week practical assessment designed to determine whether participants are ready to advance to the Technical Bootcamp. It is not:

  • An introductory course.
  • A traditional technical interview.
  • A final certification stage.
  • A requirement for everyone to do the same kind of work.

Participants enter after completing the Foundations and are expected to apply what they learned. The Challenge assesses the ability to turn a real opportunity into a documented, testable and defensible solution, working within each participant's specialization.

9.1Team structure

Teams usually consist of 2 technical participants and 2 strategic or complementary participants. The composition can be adjusted to the cohort size, the challenge requirements and the participants' capabilities.

Each team receives a real-world case or opportunity in areas such as:

  • AI applications and autonomous agents.
  • AI infrastructure and RAG systems.
  • Financial services and process optimization.
  • Commerce and intelligent automation.
  • Operational impact, education or health.
  • Other challenges in the technology ecosystem.

The goal is not to produce a commercial product ready for mass production, but to show that the team can understand a real problem, develop an appropriate solution, validate its assumptions and provide enough evidence of execution and individual contribution.

Section 10Roles and responsibilities

10.1Developers or technical participants

Developers do not need to become business specialists. They must understand the opportunity well enough to design technically relevant solutions and communicate their architecture decisions.

10.2Strategic or complementary participants

They should not reproduce the developers' technical work. Their value lies in their specialization and in their ability to translate real needs into actionable requirements, validation, quality criteria and product decisions.

10.3Shared responsibilities

Both profiles are responsible for understanding the problem, taking part in solution design, validating results, documenting together, communicating and presenting, keeping evidence of their contribution and defending it individually.

11.2Pre-Challenge

Before the three weeks of execution, the program team must:

  • Confirm participant readiness.
  • Form interdisciplinary teams.
  • Assign challenges and explain the challenge briefs.
  • Confirm the evaluation criteria and the formula.
  • Complete onboarding to the tools.
  • Create repositories and shared documents.
  • Assign technical and domain mentors.
  • Explain the evidence, version control and authorship requirements.
  • Confirm the Demo Day date and the submission cutoff.

11.3Week 1: detect, understand and scope

Objective: a clear understanding of the problem, the opportunity, the users, the requirements, the constraints, the risks and the initial hypothesis for the AI solution.

The team understands the assigned case, identifies the problem, researches the user and market context, defines assumptions, sets functional and non-functional requirements, develops the solution hypothesis and defines how it will validate it.

Evaluation gate 1: can the team clearly explain what problem it is solving, for whom, why it matters, what AI constraints exist and what evidence supports its initial direction?

11.4Week 2: build and test

Objective: turn the hypothesis into a working AI-driven prototype and start testing its assumptions and constraints, including hallucination detection and data tests.

The team implements software and agents, integrates models and tools (MCP), designs RAG flows, tests and debugs, refines the requirements and gathers validation evidence.

Evaluation gate 2: has the team shown it can go beyond a proposal and produce solid evidence of technical execution, prototyping and AI validation?

11.5Week 3: finalize, defend and evaluate

Objective: finalize the integrated prototype, organize the evidence, complete the final tests and prepare each participant for their individual defense.

Final deliverables
  1. Working code repository or validated AI prototype.
  2. Technical and architecture documentation.
  3. Opportunity and problem analysis.
  4. Strategic or operational report.
  5. Validation evidence and model stress tests.
  6. Development history and Git change control.
  7. Live demo (Demo Day).
  8. Individual contribution log.

Final evaluation gate: can each participant demonstrate a meaningful contribution, explain how the AI system works, defend their decisions and provide enough evidence of authorship and understanding?

11.6Submission cutoff

The program sets an official submission date and time before Demo Day. Changes after the cutoff are only accepted through a documented exceptions process, to preserve a fair evaluation point.

11.7Mentors during the Challenge

  • A technical mentor for AI architecture, code, agents and MCP servers.
  • A strategic or domain mentor for product, research, business analysis or user flows.
  • At least one scheduled session per week per team.

Mentors guide and play an advisory role: they do not build the project for the team or decide final grades.

Section 12AI Challenge framework

The challenge framework defines how projects are designed, assigned, executed and maintained. This book sets the common standard; a separate Project Catalog contains each challenge brief and can evolve between cohorts without changing the evaluation standard.

12.2Standard challenge brief

Each challenge must define:

  1. Project summary.
  2. Challenge statement.
  3. Context.
  4. Target users and key stakeholders.
  5. Expected outcome.
  6. Technical route.
  7. Complementary or strategic route.
  8. Required deliverables.
  9. Validation requirements.
  10. Constraints (technical, privacy, cost or latency).
  11. Demo requirements.
  12. Dependencies and assumptions.
  13. What is implemented, simulated and proposed.

12.3Challenge design principles

  • Real problems: the challenge represents a real problem, opportunity or operating scenario, not an arbitrary technical exercise.
  • Interdisciplinary execution: meaningful work for both profiles.
  • Appropriate scope: the minimum must be achievable in three weeks; optional extensions accommodate more advanced teams without being mandatory.
  • Required, recommended and optional: the brief clearly distinguishes the three levels.
  • Simulation where appropriate: if sensitive real data, production infrastructure or costly dependencies cannot be used safely, simulation or synthetic data is used, with labels for what is implemented, simulated and proposed.

12.4Catalog examples

  • Agentic assistant with RAG and MCP for institutional document management.
  • Intelligent automation and triage system for citizen service or support.
  • AI-driven adaptive tutoring and educational assessment platform.
  • Predictive analytics and operational anomaly detection pipeline.

12.5Assignment and complexity

Assignment considers the participants' specialization, team composition, complexity, learning objectives, prerequisites, mentor capacity, compute and API infrastructure, and institutional partner requirements.

LevelDescriptionTypical outcome
FoundationalLimited integration and a clearly scoped problem.Working prototype and basic validation.
IntermediateSeveral components (for example, RAG + MCP tools) or stronger validation requirements.Integrated prototype and empirical evidence.
AdvancedGreater technical, agentic, operational or domain complexity.Deep integration, rigorous validation and complete documentation.

Complexity determines scope and the support needed; it does not automatically determine a participant's grade. The common rubric applies to all projects: the evidence may vary, but the evaluation dimensions remain comparable.

12.6Catalog governance and evolution

The program periodically reviews challenges for relevance, educational value, feasibility in three weeks, mentor availability, infrastructure, evaluation clarity, participant feedback and partner requirements. A challenge can be added, updated, rescoped, reclassified, suspended or retired. New challenges are only introduced when there is enough mentoring, infrastructure, evaluation clarity and capacity.

Section 13Evidence, authorship and responsible AI use

13.1Transparent verification principle

Participants must keep an auditable record of how their work developed throughout the Challenge.

Commit or document-edit frequency must not be treated as an isolated productivity metric. Evaluation considers quality, relevance, evolution, meaningful contribution, understanding and authorship.

13.2Authorship standard

A participant must be able to explain the work attributed to them. Last-minute bulk uploads, copied material, unexplained work or work the participant cannot defend count against their individual evaluation. Authorship is established by combining process evidence with understanding shown live, not by commit volume.

13.3Responsible AI use

AI tools may be used responsibly for research, brainstorming, code generation, debugging, documentation, prototyping, analysis and workflow automation.

AI assistance does not replace individual competence. Participants must be able to explain the outputs they use, identify limitations, verify information, test generated code, explain their decisions, show a meaningful contribution and disclose AI use when the program requires it.

13.4Evidence and accountability for AI use

The program may require participants to document AI-assisted work when it materially affects the solution. The question is not whether AI was used, but whether the participant can verify, understand and take responsibility for the result.

Section 14Evaluation framework

The AI Challenge combines a team evaluation and an individual one. They are kept separate, because individual competence cannot be inferred from team performance alone.

14.1Team evaluation: 60%

Team criterionWeight
Understanding of the problem and ecosystem context10%
Opportunity identification and value definition10%
Solution quality and architectural rigor10%
Execution, functionality and technical completeness15%
Empirical validation and user feedback5%
Quality of documentation and public presentation10%
Total team score60%

14.2Individual evaluation: 40%

The individual component is evaluated according to the participant's specialization.

14.3Final score

Final score = (team × 60%) + (individual × 40%)

Both scores are on a 0 to 100 scale before weights are applied. Example: team 80 and individual 90 give a final score of 84.

14.4Minimum individual safeguard

So that nobody advances only because of strong team performance, the participant must score at least 60/100 on the individual evaluation, in addition to reaching the overall threshold. A high team score does not make up for missing individual understanding and authorship. Conversely, a high individual score preserves recognition of personal performance even if the team underperforms, but it does not remove the team component.

14.5Evaluation evidence standard

Evaluators consider work quality, relevance of the contribution, its evolution, understanding, authorship, validation, defense performance, and documentation and traceability. Commit counts, edit counts, logged hours or tool use are not used as isolated indicators.

14.6Calibration

Before scoring, evaluators review the rubric and the evidence standard together. When several evaluators score the same participant or team, they use a common rubric and documented criteria to reduce unnecessary variation.

14.7Re-evaluation and appeals

A participant may request a review when they believe a documented scoring or procedural error occurred. The review works on the evidence already submitted; it is not a chance to deliver a new project after the cutoff. The program must set the appeal deadline, the responsible person or body, the required evidence, the review period and the final decision authority.

Section 15Individual defense protocol

Each participant must be able to explain what the team built, what they personally contributed, why specific decisions were made, how their work developed, what problems they hit and how they solved them, what assumptions were validated or rejected and what limitations remain.

The goal is to verify understanding and authorship, not the amount of work delivered.

Section 17Demo Day

The AI Challenge ends with a live demo and individual questioning.

Demo Day components
  • Live demo.
  • Team presentation.
  • Technical or product explanation.
  • Individual questioning.
  • Panel evaluation.

The demo must provide evidence of:

  • What was built or prototyped.
  • How it works.
  • What assumptions were tested.
  • What the team learned.
  • What each participant contributed.

A presentation alone is not enough when the project requires functional evidence.

Section 18Final selection

The AI Challenge ends with a formal selection decision to advance to the Technical Bootcamp, based on the documented results, the readiness requirements and the approved capacity rules.

18.1Competency threshold

The reference threshold to advance is 70/100 on the final score, calculated with the 60% team + 40% individual formula. It is a readiness standard, not a claim that the participant masters the Bootcamp's advanced competencies.

18.2Individual minimum

In addition to the 70/100 threshold, the participant must score at least 60/100 on the individual evaluation.

18.3Capacity

Bootcamp places may be limited by instructor and mentor capacity, compute infrastructure and licenses or APIs, budget, technical resources and cohort size. Capacity should be defined before final selection whenever possible.

18.4Capacity-based selection rule

If more participants reach the threshold than there are places, the Selection Committee applies pre-set tie-break criteria, not improvised judgment:

  1. AI Challenge final score.
  2. Individual score.
  3. Review of evidence and authorship, if a documented tie persists.
  4. Any additional criterion explicitly approved before evaluation starts.

The criteria must be communicated before the selection process.

18.5Cases requiring a special decision

  • Above 70 overall but below 60 individual: does not meet the minimum progression rule.
  • Below 70 overall with strong individual performance: does not meet the overall threshold, unless a formally approved exception process exists.
  • Ties around the capacity limit: the tie-break sequence applies.
  • Exceptional circumstances affecting evidence delivery: the documented exceptions process applies, without informally changing the rubric.

18.6Decision authority

The Evaluation Panel produces evidence-based scores and recommendations. The Selection Committee confirms the final decision under the approved rules. The Academic Advisory Committee provides academic oversight and approves the framework and material changes to its selection rules.

18.7Selection record

The program keeps a record with: participant identifier, team score, individual score, final score, threshold status, capacity status, selection decision, approved exceptions and decision date.
