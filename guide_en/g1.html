F1.1Why AI and why now

Artificial intelligence is not new: the term was coined in 1956 and for decades it advanced in leaps, with periods of enthusiasm and “winters” of little funding. What changed in recent years was the combination of three ingredients that finally arrived together:

  • Data: the internet produced huge amounts of text, images and code to train models on.
  • Compute: graphics processors (GPUs), designed for video games, turned out to be ideal for the math neural networks need.
  • Algorithms: in 2017 the Transformer architecture appeared, making it possible to train much larger models; it is the basis of today's language models.

That inflection point means tasks that used to need a team of specialists, such as summarizing documents, classifying requests, translating or writing code, can now be prototyped in days. The opportunity is not only in creating new models, but in applying well the ones that already exist to concrete problems of institutions, businesses and people.

Key ideas
  • Data + compute + algorithms: the three explain the recent leap.
  • Most of the value is in application, not in training models from scratch.
Think about itWhich process in your work or community depends today on reading, classifying or summarizing a lot of information by hand?

F1.2Recognizing intelligent systems around you

You already use AI every day, even if you don't call it that. Learning to see it helps you imagine where else it could help:

  • Recommendation: what social networks, online stores and video platforms show you.
  • Classification: the spam filter, card fraud detection, support ticket prioritization.
  • Perception: face unlock, license plate recognition, the text your phone reads in a photo.
  • Language: conversational assistants, machine translation, voice dictation.
  • Prediction: inventory demand, machine maintenance, arrival times.

For each system you can ask three questions: what goes in (data)?, what comes out (a decision, a number, a text)? and who checks if it is wrong?

Key ideas
  • Almost every AI system does one of five things: recommend, classify, perceive, generate language or predict.
  • Input, output and oversight: that is enough to describe any system.
Think about itPick a system you used today. What would happen if it got something wrong and nobody noticed?

F1.3Diagnosing model limits and failures

A language model does not “know” things the way a database does. It learned patterns of how text is written and generates the most likely continuation. That is why it can produce fluent, confident answers that are false: this is called a hallucination.

The most common failures you will meet:

  • Invented facts: quotes, figures, laws or references that don't exist.
  • Outdated information: the model only knows what was in its training data.
  • Calculations and logic: mistakes in multi-step operations if it doesn't use a tool.
  • Bias: answers that reflect imbalances in the data it was trained on.
  • Prompt sensitivity: the same question in other words can get a different answer.

When not to use AI? When the cost of an error is high and nobody reviews it, when a simple rule solves the problem, when there is no quality data or when the decision requires legal responsibility that nobody can delegate.

Key ideas
  • Fluency is not accuracy: always verify what matters.
  • A good design decision can be “no AI here”.
Think about itWhat error rate would you accept in a system that sorts emails? And in one that suggests medical diagnoses?

F1.4Analytical decomposition and root cause

Many AI projects fail because they solve a symptom. “Citizens complain that the procedure takes too long” is a symptom; the cause could be a confusing form, documents requested twice or a bottleneck in a single person who reviews everything. AI helps with some of those causes and not with others.

Two simple tools:

  • The five whys: ask “why?” about the problem and then about each answer, until you reach something that can be changed.
  • Problem tree: the central problem in the trunk, causes in the roots and effects in the branches. Each cause becomes a possible line of solution.

In the end you should be able to write the problem in one sentence with who experiences it, what happens, how much it costs (in time, money or quality) and why it happens.

Key ideas
  • Symptom: what you see. Cause: what, if you change it, makes the symptom go away.
  • Problem first, technology second.
Think about itTake a problem you know well. Which of its causes would a good AI system solve, and which would it not?

F1.5The builder philosophy

Writing good prompts is useful, but it is not the goal of CUBO_ai. A prompt crafter asks a model and copies the answer. A builder understands the whole system: where the data comes from, what tools the model uses, how you measure whether it is right, what happens when it fails and who oversees it.

The difference shows in the questions you ask yourself:

  • Instead of “which prompt do I use?”, “what input, what output and how do I know it's right?”.
  • Instead of “it worked in my test”, “in how many cases does it work and in which does it fail?”.
  • Instead of “the model said so”, “what source do I check it against?”.

This way of thinking applies equally to technical and complementary profiles. Whoever defines good test cases and quality criteria is also building.

Key ideas
  • Builder = system + data + measurement + oversight.
  • Quality criteria are part of the product, not paperwork.
Think about itWhat would have to happen for you to trust an AI assistant to answer your institution's queries without human review?

F1 · Self-assessmentCheck what you learned

What three ingredients explain the recent progress of AI?

Large amounts of data, accessible compute (especially GPUs) and better algorithms, especially the Transformer architecture (2017).

What is a hallucination?

A model response that sounds convincing but is false or unsupported, because the model generates likely text rather than looking up facts.

Give two situations where AI is not a good idea.

When a simple rule solves the problem; when an error is serious and nobody will review it; when there is no quality data; when the decision requires responsibility that cannot be delegated.

How does a symptom differ from a cause?

The symptom is what is visible; the cause is what, if corrected, makes the symptom disappear.

What sets a builder apart from a prompt crafter?

The builder understands and designs the whole system: data, tools, quality measurement, failure handling and oversight, not just the question to the model.

DIY WORKSHOP · F1

Problem map and reliability test

Break down a real operational problem and measure how reliable a language model would be in helping to solve it. Produces the F1 deliverable.

3 hoursIndividual or in pairsTechnical: Docker + Ollama
Technical route
Measure the hallucination rate of a local model
  1. Create a cuboai-f1 repository on GitHub and clone it.
  2. Start Ollama in Docker and download a small model: 
    docker run -d -v ollama:/root/.ollama -p 11434:11434 --name ollama ollama/ollama
docker exec -it ollama ollama pull llama3.2:1b
docker exec -it ollama ollama run llama3.2:1b "What is the capital of El Salvador?"
  3. Choose an operational problem and write in questions.csv ten questions on its topic whose correct answer you know, with columns question,correct_answer.
  4. Write test_model.py, which sends each question to the local API and saves the answer: 
    import csv, requests
URL = "http://localhost:11434/api/chat"
with open("questions.csv") as f, open("results.csv", "w", newline="") as out:
    w = csv.writer(out); w.writerow(["question", "expected", "model"])
    for row in csv.DictReader(f):
        r = requests.post(URL, json={"model": "llama3.2:1b", "stream": False,
            "messages": [{"role": "user", "content": row["question"]}]})
        w.writerow([row["question"], row["correct_answer"],
                    r.json()["message"]["content"]])
  5. Review results.csv and mark each answer as correct, incomplete or hallucinated. Calculate the percentage of each.
  6. In the README, draw the problem tree with a Mermaid diagram and write where you would and would not use the model, with your percentages as evidence.
Complementary route
Problem tree and reliability matrix
  1. Choose a real operational problem from an institution, business or community you know.
  2. Apply the five whys and draw the problem tree: central problem, causes and effects.
  3. For each cause, fill in a table: does AI help? (yes, partly, no), what would it do?, what happens if it is wrong?, who would review it?
  4. Test an AI assistant with ten questions on the topic whose answer you know. Record how many it gets right, how many are incomplete and how many it invents.
  5. Write a one-page conclusion: where the model is reliable, where it is not and what human control you would add.
